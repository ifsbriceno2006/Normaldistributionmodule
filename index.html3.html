From a bell curve to a probability

The normal distribution,
one step at a time.

Explore the 68–95–99.7 rule, turn measurements into z-scores, read a normal table, and work backwards from a percentage.

Learn · Sketch · Calculate · Explain

Begin with the 68–95–99.7 rule

Imagine many measurements from a similar group of patients. If the measurements follow a normal model, most cluster near the average and fewer lie far away. Their smooth model has a bell shape.

The mean, μ (say “mew”), is the centre. The standard deviation, σ (say “sigma”), describes the spread in the same units as the measurement. A larger σ means a wider bell.

The empirical rule gives approximate percentages for a normal distribution. It does not apply automatically to every medical measurement.

Explore a simulated pulse-rate model

Assume pulse rates in a teaching dataset are normal with μ = 76 beats/min and σ = 6 beats/min. Choose how far to move from the centre.

DistanceIntervalInsideEach outside tail
μ ± σ70 to 82 bpm≈ 68%≈ 16%
μ ± 2σ64 to 88 bpm≈ 95%≈ 2.5%
μ ± 3σ58 to 94 bpm≈ 99.7%≈ 0.15%

Why divide by two? Symmetry shares the outside area equally between the left and right tails. For two SDs: 100% − 95% = 5% outside, so each tail contains 2.5%.

Useful pieces from the mean outward: 34%, 13.5%, 2.35%, then 0.15% beyond three SDs, on each side.

See each piece of the bell

Read outwards from the dotted centre line on either side. Each label belongs to the coloured strip directly below it.

34% = 68% ÷ 2; 13.5% = (95% − 68%) ÷ 2; 2.35% = (99.7% − 95%) ÷ 2; 0.15% = (100% − 99.7%) ÷ 2.

Shaded centre: approximately 95% between 64 and 88 bpm.
Out of 200 measurements: 200 × 0.95 = 190 expected inside; 200 × 0.05 = 10 expected outside, about 5 in each tail.

A count is an estimate

For 200 simulated pulse measurements, the expected count from 64 to 88 bpm is 200 × 0.95 = 190. An actual sample may contain a different number. Multiply the proportion by the group size, and round only when reporting an approximate whole-person count.

All medical scenarios in this module are hypothetical teaching models. The model cutoffs are not diagnostic thresholds.

What does the curve mean?

Four essential properties

  1. One smooth, bell-shaped peak.
  2. Symmetric around its mean.
  3. Mean = median = mode at the centre.
  4. Total area = 1, with 0.5 on either side of the mean.

The tails approach the horizontal axis without touching it. The normal model extends indefinitely in both directions.

Area is probability

The height of the curve is a density, not a probability. Probability is the area under the curve over an interval.

For a continuous measurement P(X = one exact value) = 0. Therefore P(X < a) = P(X ≤ a). Measured values may be rounded, but the mathematical model is continuous.

We write X ∼ N(μ, σ²). The second number in this notation is the variance, σ², not σ.

Always sketch first: mark the mean, mark the boundary or boundaries, shade the requested region, then decide whether your answer should be smaller or larger than 0.5.

Which shapes could reasonably fit a normal model?

A bell shape suggests a normal approximation; it does not prove exact normality. Strong skew, two peaks or a flat shape call for a different model.

Three ways to present data—and calculate its summaries

We will calculate mean, median and mode, then range, interquartile range (IQR), variance and standard deviation. Examples 1 and 2 describe exactly the same 14 patients. Example 3 uses a different, grouped dataset.

Convention for these worked examples: treat the observations as a sample, so variance is s² and standard deviation is s, using n − 1. The μ and σ on the normal curves elsewhere are parameters of the assumed normal model. They are not sample statistics.

Data summaries do not require a normal distribution. We do not draw a normal probability curve for these descriptive datasets because normality has not been assumed.

What do the measures describe?

Mean: the arithmetic average. Median: the middle of ordered data. Mode: the most frequent value. Range: maximum minus minimum. IQR: Q₃ − Q₁, the spread of the middle 50%. Variance: average squared spread with the sample correction. SD: square root of variance, back in the original units.

Quartile convention: for individual and frequency-table data, use the inclusive method (Excel/Sheets QUARTILE.INC). The position is 1 + (n − 1)p, where p = 0.25 or 0.75; interpolate when the position is between observations. Grouped quartiles use class interpolation and are estimates.
Example 1 · List of values

Clinic follow-up visits

A sample of 14 patients made the following numbers of follow-up visits during a year. The ordered data are:

1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 3, 3, 7, 7, 7, 7, 8, 8.

There are n = 14 observations, Σx = 64, and Σx² = 378.

Measures of central tendency

x̄ = Σxn
x̄ = 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 8 + 814 = 64/14 = 32/7 ≈ 4.5714 visits

Median: n is even. Average the 7th and 8th ordered observations; both are 3.

Median = 3 + 32 = 3 visits

Mode = 3 visits, occurring five times.

Measures of dispersion

Range = 8 − 1 = 7 visits.

Q₁ position = 1 + 13(0.25) = 4.25; the 4th and 5th values are both 3, so Q₁ = 3.
Q₃ position = 1 + 13(0.75) = 10.75; the 10th and 11th values are both 7, so Q₃ = 7.

IQR = Q₃ − Q₁ = 7 − 3 = 4 visits

The original 30 waiting times are unknown. Replacing every observation in a class by its midpoint gives estimates of the mean, variance and SD. Median, quartiles and mode use interpolation assumptions within classes.

Estimated mean

m = lower boundary + upper boundary2; for 10–20, m = 15 min
x̄ (estimate) = ΣfmΣf = 1110/30 = 37 min

Estimated median

Median position = n/2 = 15. The cumulative count is 8 before the 30–40 class and 18 after it, so the median lies in that class. Let L be its lower boundary, C the cumulative frequency before the class, f the class frequency and h the class width.

Median (estimate) = (n/2 − C)hf + L
Median (estimate) = (15 − 8) × 1010 + 30 = 37 min

This divides the observations within the class proportionally. It assumes they are spread evenly within the class for interpolation.

Estimated mode

The modal class is 30–40 minutes because its frequency 10 is largest. With equal-width classes, use L = 30, h = 10, f₁ = 10 (modal class), f₀ = 6 (previous class), f₂ = 8 (next class).

Mode (estimate) = (f₁ − f₀)h2f₁ − f₀ − f₂ + L
Mode (estimate) = (10 − 6) × 1020 − 6 − 8 + 30 ≈ 36.6667 min

Range: what can we actually know?

The exact range = maximum − minimum cannot be recovered from these grouped counts. The lowest occupied class begins at 10 and the highest ends at 60. Their class-boundary span is 60 − 10 = 50 min; it is not the exact observed range. If you use the midpoint approximation, its range is 55 − 15 = 40 min.

Estimated quartiles and IQR

Q₁ position = n/4 = 7.5, in the 20–30 class: L = 20, C = 2, f = 6, h = 10.

Q₁ (estimate) = (7.5 − 2) × 106 + 20 ≈ 29.1667 min

Q₃ position = 3n/4 = 22.5, in the 40–50 class: L = 40, C = 18, f = 8, h = 10.

Q₃ (estimate) = (22.5 − 18) × 108 + 40 = 45.625 min
IQR (estimate) = 45.625 − 29.1667 ≈ 16.4583 min

s² (estimate) = Σf(m − x̄)²Σf − 1
s² (estimate) = 368029 ≈ 126.8966 min²
s (estimate) = Σf(m − x̄)²Σf − 1 ≈ 11.2648 min

Estimated sample variance and SD: computational formulas

s² (estimate) = Σfm² − (Σfm)²ΣfΣf − 1
s² (estimate) = 44,750 − 1110²3029 = 3680/29 ≈ 126.8966 min²

If these observations were an entire population instead

Use μ for the population mean; divide the squared-deviation total by N, not N − 1. The population SD is the square root of that population variance. The mean, median, mode, range and IQR do not change just because we call the dataset a population.

σ² = Σ(x − μ)²N
σ = Σx² − (Σx)²NN

For a frequency table, weight each term by f. For grouped data, use midpoints and retain the “estimate” label.

Worked example 1 · one side of the mean

Recovery times are normally distributed with μ = 90 min and σ = 15 min. What proportion lasts less than 90 min?

1 · Locate 90It is exactly the mean, at the dotted symmetry line.
2 · Shade the requested side“Less than” means shade to the left.
3 · Use symmetryHalf of all area is to the left: P(X < 90) = 0.50 = 50%.

Worked example 2 · a central interval

For the same model, what proportion lasts 75 to 105 min?

1 · Compare each boundary with the mean90 − 75 = 15; 105 − 90 = 15. Each is one SD from the mean.
2 · Shade the middleThe interval is μ − σ to μ + σ.
3 · Apply the empirical ruleApproximately 0.68 = 68% lies in that central interval.

Worked example 3 · an outside tail

What proportion lasts less than 60 min?

1 · Count SDs from the mean90 − 60 = 30 = 2 × 15. The boundary is two SD below μ.
2 · Find the total outside areaAbout 95% is within two SD, so 5% is outside.
3 · Take only the requested tailThe left tail is half of 5%: 2.5% = 0.025.

The mean divides the entire curve into two equal halves, each with area 0.5. The central 68% straddles the mean, so only half of that central region—34%—lies from the mean to one SD above it.

From a normal model to the standard normal table

Normal distribution of XStandard normal distribution of Z
One of a whole family of bell curves.The one special bell curve used by the table.
Mean μ and SD σ depend on the measurements.Mean 0 and SD 1.
x has original units, such as mmHg or minutes.z counts standard deviations and has no units.
Example: μ = 125 mmHg, σ = 8 mmHg.125 mmHg becomes z = 0; 133 mmHg becomes z = 1.
A normal distribution is a probability model. A standard normal distribution is the model with mean 0 and SD 1. The standard normal table is a list of areas under that special curve; it is a lookup tool, not a different kind of data.

Original measurement scale

Shaded: 125 to 133 mmHg.

Standardised scale

Same area: z = 0 to z = 1. Only the horizontal scale changes.

Put any normal measurement on one scale

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean. Subtracting μ measures the gap; dividing by σ expresses that gap in standard-deviation units.

z = (x − μ) / σ

x = measurement; μ = population/model mean; σ = population/model SD, always positive. z has no units. A positive z is above the mean; a negative z is below it; z = 0 is the mean.

If X ∼ N(μ, σ²), then Z = (X − μ)/σ has mean 0 and SD 1: the standard normal distribution.

Worked example · simulated systolic blood pressure

Assume μ = 125 mmHg and σ = 8 mmHg. Find the z-score for 133 mmHg.

1 · Name the valuesx = 133, μ = 125, σ = 8.
2 · Subtract the mean133 − 125 = 8 mmHg above the mean.
3 · Divide by the SDz = 8/8 = 1. The measurement is one SD above the mean.

Worked example · P(125 < X < 131.24)

Let X be a hypothetical blood-pressure measurement, μ = 125 mmHg and σ = 8 mmHg. Standardise: P(0 < Z < 0.78).

  1. Shade from the mean, 0, to 0.78.
  2. The entire right half is 0.5000.
  3. The table gives the unwanted tail beyond 0.78: 0.2177.
  4. Subtract: 0.5000 − 0.2177 = 0.2823 = 28.23%.

Worked example · P(126.84 < X < 131.24)

Using the same model, standardise: P(0.23 < Z < 0.78).

  1. Shade only the band from 0.23 to 0.78.
  2. The larger tail, beyond 0.23, has area 0.4090.
  3. The smaller tail, beyond 0.78, has area 0.2177.
  4. Remove the smaller tail from the larger: 0.4090 − 0.2177 = 0.1913 = 19.13%.

Both z-values are positive, so both boundaries lie to the right of the dotted line at z = 0.

From a percentage to a measurement

So far we started with x and found an area. Now the area is given and we need x. The same formula can be rearranged:

x = μ + zσ
Top 30% means a right-tail area of 0.30. Bottom 30% means a left-tail area of 0.30. The sketch determines the sign of z; the table alone does not.

Worked example · minimum blood pressure of the highest 30%

Use μ = 125 mmHg and σ = 8 mmHg. The requested cutoff has 30% to its right.

1 · Sketch and choose the sideThe upper 30% is less than half the area, so the cutoff is above μ and z is positive.
2 · Search the body of the tableFind the entry nearest 0.3000. It is 0.3015 in row 0.5, column .02. Thus z ≈ 0.52. The nearby 0.2981 at 0.53 is slightly farther away.
3 · Return to original unitsx = 125 + 0.52(8) = 129.16 mmHg. This is the approximate minimum of the highest 30% in this model.

For the lowest 30%, symmetry gives z ≈ −0.52 and x = 125 − 0.52(8) = 120.84 mmHg.

When the mean is unknown

A simulated saline filling process has σ = 0.5 g. Choose μ so 85% of fills exceed 500 g.

μ = x − zσ
  1. P(X > 500) = 0.85, so P(X < 500) = 0.15.
  2. The 500 g cutoff is below the mean, so z is negative.
  3. Closest table entry to 0.15 is 0.1492, so z ≈ −1.04.
  4. μ = 500 − (−1.04)(0.5) = 500.52 g.

Double negative matters: the mean must be greater than 500 g to put 85% of the distribution above 500 g.
